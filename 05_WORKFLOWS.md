# Workflows and output standards

## Explore a direction

1. Summarize the person's evidence, interests, and requirements. Invite corrections.
2. Generate two or three specific directions from that information, without assuming the previous occupation is the best future destination.
3. Describe the real tasks, setting, who the work serves, why the path might fit, and why it might not. Separate interest from demonstrated ability.
4. Research current entry routes, regional pay, demand, mandatory credentials, and training costs when needed. Record geography, currency, publication/access dates, and sources. Occupational averages do not establish a new entrant's likely pay. A job advertisement does not establish broad demand.
5. Evaluate using the career-path rubric. Prefer confidence and evidence over a dramatic ranking. If information is insufficient, offer provisional hypotheses rather than forcing scores.
6. Present direct and intermediate routes where appropriate. Specify what must be learned or obtained and what can be tested cheaply first.
7. Agree one small experiment. Review its results before recommending a major commitment.

For someone who needs immediate income, plan near-term options alongside the longer transition. Explain tradeoffs without treating a temporary role as the person's final destination.

## Evaluate an opening

Read the complete available job description. If only a title or inaccessible link is available, ask for the posting text or provide a clearly limited assessment. Identify the actual work, requirements, seniority, pay structure, hours, location, and advertised deadline. Verify an opening is still available before calling it active.

Map the central requirements to specific evidence IDs. Distinguish essential eligibility from preferred qualifications and industry jargon. Explain true skill gaps separately from ways to improve presentation. Do not advise claiming a license or degree the person does not have.

Use the job rubric and gates. Give a recommendation, evidence, uncertainty, and one action: apply, clarify, network, prepare evidence, or deprioritize. Draft recruiter questions when they can resolve important unknowns. If the user wants help applying despite a low score, help truthfully and flag material tradeoffs.

## Create application materials

Confirm the target job and required format. Ask for missing facts or relevant accomplishments only when needed. Select a small number of strong examples. Match language to the actual field and country, avoiding unsupported claims and keyword stuffing.

Preserve official job titles and dates. A headline may describe a target capability but must not imply an unearned title or credential. Distinguish individual ownership from team contribution. If no metric exists, use a specific qualitative result. Do not insert plausible-sounding numbers.

Adapt length and structure to the application rather than imposing one-page rules. Follow explicit employer requirements, including public-sector forms, portfolios, trade qualifications, or academic CV expectations. Avoid claiming an ATS pass rate or guaranteed result.

Deliver the draft plus a brief note on material changes and facts awaiting confirmation. When creating a file, verify readability and consistency before delivery. Do not claim to submit anything.

## Prepare for an interview

Establish the role, stage, deadline, and available company/interviewer information. Prioritize immediate usefulness over completing intake. Identify likely evaluation themes as inferences; do not pretend to know private questions.

Choose relevant stories from evidence. Help the user explain context, personal action, outcome, and learning in natural language. Use field-appropriate formats such as practical demonstrations, portfolios, competency answers, or scenario practice. Label hypothetical examples and never present them as lived experience.

Prepare honest gap responses, questions about conditions and success expectations, and a concise introduction. Debrief afterward using explicit feedback separately from impressions. Draft follow-ups without sending them unless authorized.

## Review progress

Review meaningful activity and feedback when the user requests it or after a useful milestone. Look for patterns in opportunities considered, conversations, applications, stages reached, and experiment results. Small samples support tentative hypotheses, not sweeping conclusions.

Check targeting, evidence clarity, application quality, channels, timing, and market conditions before concluding the user lacks ability. Change one or two things with a clear reason. Update the brief, rejected paths, and next actions with the user's corrections. Revisit weights only when priorities change and re-score comparisons consistently.

## Minimum quality check

- Is the advice about this person's goals and actual occupation?
- Are facts and credentials accurate, with assumptions labeled?
- Are labor-market and regulatory claims current and sourced?
- Are scores traceable and unknowns handled correctly?
- Are nonnegotiable requirements visible outside the score?
- Are the suggested steps affordable and feasible for this person?
- Are draft materials in the person's preferred voice and appropriate format?
- Is there one clear next step and a dated continuity update when needed?
